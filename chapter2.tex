\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:background}

This chapter surveys prior work that underpins and contextualizes the conversational, LLM-based risk assessment pipeline introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:Introduction}. We highlight the traditional tabular baselines used for comparison, the transformer models that motivated our shift toward generative approaches, and the medical foundation models most relevant to our experiments.

\subsection{Disease Risk Prediction With Traditional and Deep Machine Learning}

Disease risk assessment has traditionally been formulated as a supervised learning problem on structured (tabular) data. Models are trained to map demographic, clinical, behavioral, and environmental features to outcomes such as disease incidence, progression, or treatment response. Classical approaches include logistic regression, which models the log-odds of the outcome as a linear function of predictors and remains a standard baseline for binary risk prediction \cite{hosmer2013applied}. Ensemble methods such as random forests and gradient-boosted trees improve upon linear models by capturing nonlinear interactions and higher-order dependencies between features \cite{breiman2001random,chen2016xgboost}.

In clinical research, deep learning has been used to extend these ideas through multitask and representation-learning frameworks. Li et al. proposed a deep multitask neural network that uses auxiliary measures to jointly model multiple related outcomes, improving prediction accuracy and stability for disease risk assessment in clinical cohorts \cite{li2018leveraging}. Earlier work by Wang et al. demonstrated how multi-level risk factor modeling can be applied to clinical risk prediction using deep neural architectures \cite{wang2019prioritization}. Building on this line of work, Li et al. introduced deep mixture neural networks that stratify patients into latent subgroups and train specialized subnetworks within each stratum to predict clinical outcomes, effectively capturing heterogeneity in patient populations \cite{li2020predicting}.

These approaches illustrate the strengths and limitations of traditional and deep machine learning for disease risk prediction. On the one hand, they can achieve high performance when trained on well-curated, large-scale tabular datasets. On the other hand, they typically require extensive feature engineering, careful handling of missing data, and disease-specific model development. Each new condition, outcome, or cohort often demands a separate training pipeline, and the resulting models are not naturally suited to interactive workflows where patients provide information incrementally or in free-text form. This motivates a shift toward architectures that can flexibly integrate heterogeneous signals and support conversational interfaces.

\subsection{Transformer-Based Encoders in Clinical Prediction}

The introduction of transformer architectures marked a major shift in natural language processing (NLP) and, by extension, in clinical NLP applications. BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) demonstrated that large-scale pretraining on general-domain text, followed by task-specific finetuning, can yield strong performance across diverse NLP benchmarks \cite{devlin2019bert}. The core idea is to learn deep contextual embeddings by predicting masked tokens and, optionally, sentence relationships, enabling downstream models to benefit from rich language representations without training from scratch.

Clinical variants of BERT were soon developed to better reflect biomedical language. ClinicalBERT and BioClinicalBERT were trained or further finetuned on corpora such as MIMIC-III clinical notes and discharge summaries, leading to improved performance on tasks such as mortality prediction, hospital readmission risk, and phenotyping \cite{huang2019clinicalbert,alsentzer2019publicly}. These models showed that domain-adaptive pretraining on clinical text can capture terminology, abbreviations, and stylistic patterns specific to electronic health records, which are often poorly represented in general-domain corpora.

Transformer-based encoders have also been applied to structured EHR data. Med-BERT adapts the BERT framework to large-scale longitudinal EHRs by treating sequences of clinical codes as tokens and learning contextual representations that support disease prediction tasks across multiple phenotypes \cite{rasmy2021medbert}. By modeling temporal dependencies and co-occurrence patterns in diagnostic and procedure codes, Med-BERT achieves substantial improvements over conventional models on several disease risk benchmarks.

Despite these advances, encoder-only transformers are primarily discriminative: they excel at tasks such as classification and sequence labeling but are less naturally suited to generative, conversational interaction. They usually require a fixed input format, do not directly generate natural-language explanations, and are not designed to support multi-turn dialogue. As a result, while transformer-based encoders have significantly improved clinical prediction from text and structured codes, they do not address the broader need for interactive, patient-facing risk assessment that can absorb free-text input and respond in natural language.

\subsection{Generative Large Language Models for Medical Reasoning and Communication}

Generative large language models extend transformer-based architectures by using decoder or encoder–decoder designs that are optimized for text generation. GPT-3 exemplified this shift by demonstrating strong zero- and few-shot performance across a wide variety of tasks, including question answering and text completion, without task-specific finetuning \cite{brown2020language}. Later models such as GPT-4 further improved reasoning capabilities and robustness, as described in its technical report \cite{achiam2023gpt}.

In medicine, generative LLMs have been evaluated on standardized examinations and clinical vignettes. ChatGPT has achieved near or above passing performance on USMLE-style questions, suggesting that large pretrained models can capture a substantial amount of medical knowledge even without explicit domain-specific finetuning \cite{kung2023performance,gilson2023chatgpt}. Nori et al. assessed GPT-4 on a range of medical challenge problems, finding that it can generate plausible diagnostic hypotheses and clinical reasoning steps but still exhibits important limitations and failure modes that require clinician oversight \cite{nori2023capabilities}. Kanjee et al. evaluated a generative AI model in a complex diagnostic challenge and showed that it can propose reasonable diagnoses, again highlighting the need for careful human verification in high-stakes settings \cite{kanjee2023accuracy}.

Beyond exam-style benchmarks, generative LLMs have been used to support patient communication and documentation. Ali et al. explored the use of ChatGPT to generate patient clinic letters from clinician inputs, demonstrating that LLMs can produce readable, patient-friendly summaries that may reduce documentation burden while improving clarity \cite{ali2023chatgpt}. Xu et al. investigated ChatGPT’s utility in urology by supplying semistructured patient data and examining the generated differential diagnoses, concluding that the model can serve as an assistive tool but should not replace specialist judgment \cite{xv2023can}.

More broadly, conversational agents have been studied as tools for health communication, mental-health support, and triage. Miner et al. evaluated smartphone-based conversational agents and their responses to questions about mental health, interpersonal violence, and physical health, highlighting both their potential and their limitations in addressing sensitive topics \cite{miner2016smartphone}. These findings collectively indicate that generative LLMs can support medical reasoning and communication tasks, but also emphasize the importance of responsible deployment, explicit scope limitations, and transparency about system capabilities.

\subsection{Medical Foundation Models and Domain-Specific Pretraining}

A growing body of work focuses on foundation models specialized for medicine. Med-PaLM and Med-PaLM 2 adapt large language models to medical domains by finetuning on curated medical question–answer data sets and using instruction tuning to align model outputs with expert expectations \cite{singhal2025toward}. These models achieve strong performance on benchmarks such as MedQA and MultiMedQA, narrowing the gap between general-purpose LLMs and human clinicians on structured QA tasks.

Open-source efforts have produced medical foundation models that can be inspected, finetuned, and deployed without relying solely on proprietary APIs. PMC-LLaMA uses PubMed Central and related biomedical corpora to pretrain a family of LLaMA-based models tailored to medical language, enabling research into domain-specific LLM behavior and adaptation \cite{wu2024pmcllama}. BioMedLM is another biomedical language model trained on scientific and clinical text to support downstream tasks such as named entity recognition, relation extraction, and document classification in the biomedical domain \cite{bolton2024biomedlm}.

ClinicalMamba extends this line of research by introducing a generative architecture designed specifically for longitudinal clinical notes, enabling improved modeling of temporal clinical patterns \cite{yang2024clinicalmamba}. PMC LLaMA provides another example of domain-specialized pretraining, using PubMed Central articles to adapt the LLaMA architecture for medical reasoning tasks \cite{wu2024pmcllama}.

ClinicalMamba extends foundation modeling to longitudinal clinical notes, training a generative model on sequences of clinical narratives to support downstream predictive tasks such as mortality and readmission risk \cite{yang2024clinicalmamba}. By modeling long-range dependencies in clinical documentation, ClinicalMamba provides a foundation for generative reasoning over patient trajectories.

These domain-specialized models illustrate a trend toward aligning model pretraining corpora and objectives with medical applications. They offer advantages in terminology coverage, robustness to domain-specific expressions, and controllability over outputs. However, many remain focused on clinician-facing tasks such as QA, summarization, and document understanding. There is comparatively less work on using medical foundation models directly for patient-facing interactive risk assessment, where conversational quality, interpretability, and user comprehension are central design concerns.

\subsection{Generative Models for Structured, Tabular, and Mixed-Modal Prediction}

While early generative LLM applications in medicine focused on pure text tasks, recent work has explored how these models can ingest and reason over structured data, such as tabular EHRs or wearable sensor readings. TabLLM showed that carefully prompting large language models with serialized representations of tabular data can yield competitive classification performance, particularly in few-shot settings where conventional models struggle due to limited training examples \cite{hegselmann2022tabllm}. This suggests that LLMs can act as flexible classifiers over structured inputs when those inputs are cast into textual form.

Health-LLM integrates conversational input and wearable sensor data to perform personalized disease prediction and health monitoring \cite{kim2024health}. Instead of training a separate model for each prediction task, Health-LLM uses a pretrained language model and uses retrieval-augmented generation to combine real-time sensor signals with background knowledge for tasks such as stress and fatigue prediction. CPLLM proposes a general framework for clinical prediction using large language models by conditioning on structured EHR-style inputs and generating outcome probabilities via language modeling objectives, demonstrating that LLMs can be competitive with traditional baselines across multiple clinical outcomes \cite{benshoham2024cpllm}.

These studies collectively argue that generative LLMs can operate as unified engines for prediction over mixed inputs—including tabular features, narratives, and conversational histories—provided that the data is serialized appropriately. For disease risk assessment, this opens the possibility of using a single model to process both structured risk factors and free-text user descriptions, and to generate not only risk estimates but also natural-language explanations. However, empirical comparisons with strong tabular baselines remain necessary to understand when and how LLMs offer advantages in prediction accuracy, calibration, and robustness.

\subsection{Instruction Tuning and Zero-/Few-Shot Generalization}

A key enabling factor for using LLMs in risk assessment without extensive task-specific finetuning is instruction tuning. Instruction-tuned models are trained on diverse collections of input–output pairs framed as instructions and responses, improving their ability to follow natural-language prompts and generalize to unseen tasks. T0 is an early example, demonstrating that multitask prompted training on many NLP benchmarks allows a single model to perform zero-shot or few-shot inference on new tasks without explicit supervision \cite{sanh2021multitask}. Flan-T5 extends this approach by scaling both the model and the instruction-tuning dataset, showing substantial gains in zero-shot performance across a wide range of benchmarks \cite{chung2024scaling}.

Instruction tuning is particularly relevant for clinical applications where labeled data is scarce, sensitive, or expensive to obtain. Instead of collecting large supervised data sets for each disease-specific risk model, developers can use instruction-tuned LLMs and design prompts that specify input formats, prediction tasks, and output constraints. This reduces the burden of data collection and enables rapid prototyping of new risk assessment tools. For cases where additional adaptation is needed, parameter-efficient finetuning methods such as LoRA can adjust a subset of model parameters while keeping the base model frozen, enabling customization under resource constraints \cite{hu2022lora}.

In the context of conversational risk assessment, instruction-tuned LLMs can be prompted to interpret serialized risk-factor data, generate probability estimates, and produce explanations in natural language without retraining from scratch. This flexibility supports the design of systems that can be updated or repurposed as guidelines evolve, provided that careful evaluation and monitoring are performed.

\subsection{LLMs for Medical Evidence Summarization and Knowledge Synthesis}

Another line of related work investigates how LLMs can assist in synthesizing medical evidence. Tang et al. evaluated large language models on the task of summarizing medical research abstracts and articles, finding that models such as GPT-4 can produce concise, clinically relevant summaries but may occasionally omit important details or introduce minor factual inconsistencies \cite{tang2023evaluating}. Such summarization capabilities could support clinicians in staying current with rapidly evolving literature, especially in fast-moving areas like COVID-19.

Sallam surveyed the potential of ChatGPT and similar models in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, considering roles in literature search, screening, data extraction, and preliminary synthesis \cite{sallam2023chatgpt}. The review emphasizes that while LLMs can reduce manual workload, they must be used with caution, under human supervision, and with explicit checks for hallucinations and misclassification. These findings highlight both the promise and the risks of relying on generative models for tasks that influence clinical or public health decisions.

For risk assessment applications, the ability of LLMs to integrate and summarize evidence is relevant in two ways. First, they can contextualize individualized risk estimates within broader epidemiological trends or guideline-based recommendations. Second, they can translate complex research findings into patient-friendly language. However, reliance on generative models for evidence synthesis also raises concerns about transparency, provenance, and reproducibility, reinforcing the need for clear boundaries around their use and robust evaluation protocols.
