\section{Conclusion}

This thesis explored the use of generative large language models (LLMs) for automated COVID-19 severity assessment using structured symptom, demographic, and comorbidity information collected through a conversational interface. The results show that, when fine-tuned with a small number of serialized examples, LLMs can outperform traditional machine-learning baselines such as logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost}. The strongest LLM configurations achieved higher AUC values and more stable performance across few-shot settings, demonstrating the efficiency of aligning pretrained language representations with structured clinical prediction tasks.

A key takeaway is that few-shot fine-tuning serves as a practical and data-efficient strategy. Even two or four examples per class were sufficient for the model to internalize how structured health features map to severity outcomes. Additionally, the choice of serialization format significantly influenced model behavior: list-based prompts offered greater stability in low-shot scenarios, while text-based prompts occasionally improved performance as more examples were introduced. This highlights that input representation is not merely a formatting choice but a core modeling decision.

We developed a system that demonstrates how predictive modeling can be integrated into a real-time conversational assessment platform. LLMs were able to generate risk predictions and explanations based on the collected information. Such capabilities extend beyond the output of conventional ML classifiers and support user engagement, transparency, and better communication of risk.

Overall, this work shows that LLMs provide an effective and adaptable foundation for digital health assessment tools. Their ability to generate explanations, adapt with minimal labeled data, and operate within interactive systems suggests a promising path forward for accessible and explainable decision support during public-health crises.
