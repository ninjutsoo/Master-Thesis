\section{Introduction}
\label{chap:Introduction}

\subsection{Background}

Disease risk assessment is a critical component of public health surveillance, where demographic variables and social determinants of health are used to estimate a patient’s susceptibility to disease, predict treatment response, and forecast severity outcomes \cite{li2018leveraging,wang2019prioritization,li2020predicting}. Traditionally, these predictions rely on machine learning models trained de novo for each disease using curated structured (tabular) data. For example, Earlier work by Wang et al. demonstrated how multi-level risk factor modeling can be applied to clinical risk prediction using deep neural architectures \cite{wang2019prioritization}, while Li et al. proposed a deep mixture neural network to stratify patients and predict heart failure risk within each subgroup \cite{li2020predicting}. These approaches can achieve strong performance but depend heavily on feature engineering, labeled tabular data sets, and bespoke model development for each condition.

The advent of transformer architectures has shifted this paradigm by enabling models that can capture complex dependencies in clinical data with minimal manual feature design. Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) introduced a pretraining–finetuning recipe that has been widely adopted in health care applications \cite{devlin2019bert}. Domain-specialized variants such as ClinicalBERT and BioClinicalBERT, trained on MIMIC-III clinical notes, improve performance on tasks like mortality prediction and readmission risk \cite{huang2019clinicalbert,alsentzer2019publicly}. Med-BERT extends this idea to large-scale structured electronic health records (EHRs) and achieves strong area under the curve (AUC) values for disease prediction across multiple phenotypes \cite{rasmy2021medbert}. Despite their success, these BERT-style models are primarily discriminative encoders optimized for classification or regression and are less naturally suited to interactive, streaming question–answer (QA) workflows that characterize conversational risk assessment tools.

In many real-world settings, risk assessment is not a one-shot prediction from a static feature vector but a dynamic process: patients provide partial information, ask clarifying questions, and update their responses over time. Conventional discriminative models struggle to ingest such mixed, evolving inputs, especially when they combine categorical responses, free text, and contextual metadata. This motivates moving from task-specific discriminative models toward more flexible, generative architectures that can both understand and produce natural language while grounding their predictions in clinical variables (Figure \ref{fig:convai_vs_traditional}).

\subsection{Generative Large Language Models for Health Care}

Large, generative language models such as GPT-3 have overcome several limitations of discriminative encoders by handling heterogeneous data formats and supporting natural language interaction \cite{brown2020language}. These models can jointly process structured clinical attributes and unstructured narratives, integrating information from patient histories, symptom descriptions, and contextual factors to support complex tasks such as disease severity prediction and triage. As a result, generative LLMs have been explored for diagnostic support, clinical decision-making, knowledge extraction, and personalized risk monitoring across a broad range of conditions.

In diagnostic support, models like ChatGPT and GPT-4 have been evaluated on standardized medical examinations and simulated clinical cases. Gilson et al. assessed ChatGPT on USMLE Step 1 and Step 2 multiple-choice questions and showed that its performance approached or exceeded the passing threshold, highlighting its potential in medical education and diagnostic reasoning \cite{gilson2023chatgpt}. Kung et al. tested ChatGPT on USMLE-style clinical vignettes to examine its capacity for clinical reasoning without medical-specific finetuning \cite{kung2023performance}. Beyond exam-style questions, Ali et al. used ChatGPT to generate patient-friendly clinical letters from semistructured physician inputs, demonstrating how LLMs can support communication and documentation \cite{ali2023chatgpt}. Xu et al. applied ChatGPT to urological diseases using semistructured patient data and found that it can produce plausible differential diagnoses, suggesting a role as an assistive tool in preliminary diagnostic workups \cite{xv2023can}. Kanjee et al. evaluated GPT-4 on a complex diagnostic challenge and showed that it can generate reasonable diagnostic hypotheses, though with important limitations that necessitate clinician oversight \cite{kanjee2023accuracy}. Complementary to these targeted evaluations, Nori et al. characterized GPT-4’s capabilities across a range of medical challenge problems, providing a broader view of its strengths and failure modes in clinical reasoning \cite{nori2023capabilities}.

Generative LLMs are also increasingly used as tools for synthesizing medical evidence. Tang et al. evaluated large language models on summarizing medical literature and showed that models like GPT-4 can generate concise, clinically relevant summaries of research articles, potentially reducing the burden of staying up to date with emerging findings \cite{tang2023evaluating}. Sallam surveyed the role of LLMs in systematic reviews and meta-analyses, outlining how they can assist in literature search, screening, and data extraction while emphasizing the need for human verification \cite{sallam2023chatgpt}. These studies suggest that generative LLMs can act as “literature co-pilots,” enabling clinicians and researchers to navigate rapidly expanding evidence bases more efficiently.

Beyond knowledge and diagnostic tasks, several recent works directly use generative LLMs for risk prediction and personalized monitoring. Health-LLM integrates conversational inputs and wearable sensor data, such as physical activity and heart rate, to predict stress, fatigue, and related health metrics in a zero-shot manner \cite{kim2024health}. Instead of training a new model for each outcome, Health-LLM leverages the generalization capabilities of a pretrained LLM to adapt to multiple prediction tasks with minimal labeled data. ClinicalMamba extends this line of research by introducing a generative architecture designed specifically for longitudinal clinical notes, enabling improved modeling of temporal clinical patterns \cite{yang2024clinicalmamba}. Together, these efforts illustrate a broader shift toward using generative LLMs as unified engines that can handle both natural language and structured signals for a variety of clinical prediction problems.

\subsection{Foundation Models Specialized for Medicine}

While general-purpose LLMs such as GPT-3 and GPT-4 exhibit strong zero-shot performance, several groups have developed foundation models specialized for medicine. Med-PaLM and Med-PaLM 2 fine-tune large language models on curated medical QA data sets and demonstrate near expert-level performance on benchmark question-answering tasks, including those derived from standardized exams and real-world clinical queries \cite{singhal2025toward}. PMC LLaMA provides another example of domain-specialized pretraining, using PubMed Central articles to adapt the LLaMA architecture for medical reasoning tasks \cite{wu2024pmcllama}.

Beyond pure text, foundation models for medicine increasingly combine structured and unstructured data. CPLLM, for example, explores clinical prediction with large language models by conditioning on structured EHR-style inputs and generating outcome probabilities through language modeling objectives \cite{benshoham2024cpllm}. Llama 2, a family of open-weight foundation models, has been widely adopted as a base for medical finetuning due to its scalability and permissive licensing \cite{touvron2023llama2}. BioMedLM is another open biomedical language model trained on large biomedical corpora and positioned as a base for downstream medical NLP tasks \cite{bolton2024biomedlm}. These specialized models provide an ecosystem of architectures that can be adapted to disease risk prediction, prognosis, and patient-facing applications.

A parallel line of work investigates how LLMs handle tabular or mixed-modality inputs. TabLLM demonstrates that modern LLMs can perform competitive inference on tabular data when provided with appropriately formatted prompts, often matching traditional models in low-data regimes \cite{hegselmann2022tabllm}. Instruction-tuned models such as T0 and Flan-T5 further show that task-agnostic finetuning across diverse NLP tasks improves zero- and few-shot generalization, including classification over structured inputs serialized into text \cite{sanh2021multitask,chung2024scaling}. These findings suggest that, with suitable prompt design, generative LLMs can operate as unified predictors over conversational histories, demographic attributes, and other structured features, which is particularly attractive for real-time, patient-facing risk assessment tools.

\subsection{Motivation and Scope of This Thesis}

Despite rapid progress, several gaps remain between the capabilities of generative LLMs and the practical requirements of disease risk assessment in real-world settings. First, many existing studies evaluate LLMs on static benchmarks or retrospective vignettes rather than on interactive, patient-facing workflows that mirror how risk assessment is conducted outside the clinic. Second, comparatively few works systematically compare generative LLMs with strong tabular baselines (eg, logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost) across varying data regimes, especially in scenarios with limited labeled data where LLMs may be particularly advantageous. Third, there is limited empirical evidence on how to operationalize LLM-powered risk assessment through deployable applications that patients can use on their own devices, while still providing clinically meaningful outputs and basic interpretability.

The COVID-19 pandemic provides a concrete and urgent testbed for addressing these gaps. During periods of high transmission and constrained clinical capacity, individuals frequently seek personalized guidance about their risk of severe disease based on symptoms, comorbidities, and contextual factors. Traditional web-based risk calculators often require users to manually fill forms and do not accommodate follow-up questions, clarifications, or narrative descriptions. A conversational interface powered by a generative LLM can, in principle, collect rich symptom and context information in natural language, map this interaction into a structured representation, and return individualized risk estimates along with explanatory feedback.

This thesis focuses on the design, implementation, and evaluation of a generative LLM–powered conversational AI application for personalized COVID-19 risk assessment. The system uses a large language model to process question–answer pairs from users, predict the probability of severe COVID-19 outcomes, and provide personalized feedback through a mobile-friendly interface. The work systematically compares the LLM-based pipeline with traditional tabular models under zero-shot and few-shot conditions, analyzes performance across different data availability regimes, and explores simple mechanisms for feature-importance–style explanations that can be surfaced to end users. In doing so, the thesis aims to provide an end-to-end case study of how generative LLMs can be integrated into a disease risk assessment workflow, from model design and evaluation to deployment in a patient-facing app.
